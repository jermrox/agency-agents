Cadets get hurt about as often as soldiers in initial training, a USARIEM-led review finds

A scoping review led by USARIEM researchers pooled 34 US studies of service academy and ROTC cadets, covering cohorts of 27 to 24,501, and put every injury rate on the same scale. Academy cadets averaged 53.7 musculoskeletal injuries per 1,000 person-weeks and ROTC cadets 30.6. Most injuries were in the lower body, and female cadets had higher rates. Only 10 of the 34 studies were rated moderate quality, so for strength coaches, PTs and ATs working with pre-commissioning programs this is a starting benchmark and a case for better injury surveillance, not a final number.

Night-owl veterans carry heavier PTSD, depression and insomnia symptoms

In 144 post-9/11 US veterans from a multi-site post-deployment health registry, evening types reported worse depression, PTSD, insomnia severity and sleep quality than morning types after adjusting for age and sex, and the link was not explained by how long they slept. Chronotype was not tied to trauma-associated sleep disturbance. It is a small cross-sectional sample, but for performance psychologists and H2F sleep staff it is a cheap screening cue: asking about sleep timing, not just duration, may flag who carries more symptom burden and who may respond differently to CBT-I.

Neuromuscular training during recruit training cuts injuries; load management alone did not

A JOSPT meta-analysis pooled 33 studies and 49,062 military, police and fire recruits. Neuromuscular training delivered during recruit training reduced medical-attention injuries without raising attrition. Programs started before training did not reduce injuries and may have increased dropout, and load management alone showed no clear benefit. The evidence is very low certainty, but it backs embedding progressive neuromuscular work inside the academy schedule instead of bolting it on beforehand.

Heavy-weapons training blast changes memory, gait and brain biomarkers within hours

The five-year INVICTA study followed 224 special operators, controls and range instructors through heavy-weapons training with blast gauges. Within minutes of firing, operators showed changes in short-term memory, EEG complexity and blood tau and UCH-L1, and gait and eye-movement changes appeared within hours. More than 20% had a hearing threshold shift. Effects were mostly short-lived, but instructors with repeated exposure over two-year tours had larger and longer-lasting changes, a direct input to exposure limits and to scheduling cadre on ranges.

Six in ten first-term Sailors and Marines on limited duty leave service early

Among 22,500 enlisted Sailors and Marines who entered temporary limited duty (LIMDU) in their first contract (FY2017-2022), more than 60% left before finishing their service obligation, most often for medical disability. Entering LIMDU for mental or behavioral health conditions predicted worse outcomes than entering for musculoskeletal injury or pain. Marines did better than Sailors. The results raise questions about how often LIMDU returns people to full duty, which is where embedded rehab and performance teams can make a difference.

Review finds too little evidence to write diet rules for shift workers, firefighters included

A systematic review of 43 studies on nutrition interventions for shift workers, with a pre-specified look at first responders, found the evidence too heterogeneous and biased to support formal dietary guidelines. Only about 9% of studies included firefighters or police; common interventions were macronutrient changes, time-restricted eating and meal timing. Dietitians working with 24-hour crews should treat shift-work nutrition advice as practice-based rather than settled, and there is room for well-designed station-based trials.

Slow ruck times at Special Forces selection flag who gets hurt, sick or separated over the next two years

USARIEM followed 792 Special Forces Assessment and Selection candidates for two years after they enrolled, whether they passed or not. Slower road march times at enrollment came with 25-66% higher odds of injury, behavioral health diagnoses, sleep disorders and separation from service. More resistance training, better sleep quality and higher protein intake came with 18-45% lower odds of injury, and low mood, poor sleep and thin social support each raised the odds of separation by about 30%. For SOF strength coaches, dietitians and performance psychologists, this backs screening and building load-carriage capacity, lifting, sleep and protein before a candidate shows up at selection.

Heavier World Trade Center exposure tracks with a thinner cortex in responders at midlife

Stony Brook researchers scanned 218 World Trade Center responders and unexposed controls aged 44 to 65 and found that a higher neurotoxicant exposure severity score went with thinner cortex across much of the brain, focused in precentral regions, even after adjusting for other factors. It is a cross-sectional imaging study and cannot prove cause, but it adds structural brain evidence to the cognitive-decline findings already reported in this group. For fire and rescue program leaders and medical staff, it strengthens the case for respiratory protection and exposure tracking at large incidents as long-term brain-health measures, not only lung and cancer measures.

Gynecomastia: 22,000 service members and $90 million in care over a decade

A Military Health System analysis found 22,103 active-duty men were diagnosed with gynecomastia from 2014 to 2023 (2.0 per 1,000 person-years, highest in the Marine Corps at 2.9). The paper does not assess causes, but it gives men's health and performance staff a baseline for a condition linked to hormones, supplements and anabolic agents in young male populations.

Airmen and Guardians with high neuroticism score lower on VO2max, Wingate and endurance tests, and autonomy explains part of the gap

Researchers from AFIT and AFRL gave 186 active-duty Air Force and Space Force members personality and motivation surveys, then measured VO2max, Wingate anaerobic power and muscular endurance around a 12-week supervised training program. Higher neuroticism went with lower scores on all three tests and higher conscientiousness went with better aerobic capacity and endurance. Of the motivation measures, only a sense of autonomy predicted performance, and once autonomy was accounted for, neuroticism no longer predicted aerobic capacity or endurance. The mediation analysis was cross-sectional and the sample was one of convenience. Even so, it gives coaches and performance psychologists a reason to coach in an autonomy-supportive way, especially with anxious trainees on maximal-effort days.

Sixteen load-carriage-experienced adults marched 9.65 km (6 miles) with a fighting load at 22 C without fluids. They lost about 1.5% of body mass and became mildly dehydrated. A job-specific firefighter task test proves reliable enough to track moderate fitness changes

Brazilian researchers with Bond University's Tactical Research Unit tested a new simulated-task circuit (vehicle extrication plus structural fire suppression) twice in 21 career firefighters. For coaches building job-related tests, it is a template for a defensible occupational test and a reminder to know a test's typical error before calling a change real.

SOF operators carry more lean mass, not less fat, than conventional units

In 500 Belgian male soldiers measured with multifrequency bioimpedance, special operations personnel had the highest fat-free mass index (21.0 kg/m2) while their fat mass index matched other units. PT instructors had the lowest body fat (13.7%) and frigate sailors the highest (19.7%). Over half of SOF combined low fat mass with high lean mass. The authors suggest lean-mass indices, not weight or BMI, as the more useful body composition marker in selection.

A meal-replacement shake plan cut body fat in overweight career firefighters

In a cluster-randomized trial across 26 fire stations (51 firefighters, average BMI 33), two high-protein meal-replacement shakes a day for 14 weeks reduced DXA body fat by about 1 percentage point versus wait-list control. Protein and fiber intake rose, trunk fat and BMI fell, and heart-rate recovery after a weighted stair climb improved, though stair-climb time and blood lipids did not change. Adherence was about 91% and crews found it convenient, which makes this a practical option dietitians and station fitness staff can actually run on shift.

Audiometry on 18 helicopter EMS crew members across 236 tests showed hearing thresholds worsening over a duty day, with the biggest shift in medical crew who wore no added hearing protection (6.8 dB) versus pilots (1.9 dB) and protected medics (0.5 dB). Soldiers with embedded H2F teams get back from MSK injuries faster, 1.4-million-injury JAMA study finds

A USARIEM-led cohort study of 1.42 million musculoskeletal injuries in active-duty soldiers (FY18 to FY25) found that soldiers in brigades with Holistic Health and Fitness teams spent 5.9% fewer days on limited duty. Ketone supplement preserved shooting performance during four nights of half sleep

In a double-blind crossover RCT, 32 adults (16 women) slept about 4 hours a night for four nights while taking a 25 g ketone precursor or placebo. The ketone drink raised blood ketones above 1.0 mM, added 13% more deep sleep, and improved virtual-reality shooting: an 8% better Hit Factor, 7% faster completion and slightly higher accuracy. The trial is small, but it gives dietitians and performance staff an early signal on a countermeasure for sleep restriction.

A five-minute daily drill cut ankle sprains by more than half in infantry recruits

In a quasi-experimental study of infantry recruit cohorts, a daily 5-minute, equipment-free balance, agility and strength routine run for 12 weeks lowered ankle sprain incidence from 31.0% to 13.1%. Injured recruits in the program also held their functional performance, while injured controls declined. The benefit was largest for recruits with a previous sprain. It is a cheap, scalable warm-up that strength coaches and ATs can drop into existing PT.

The Wildland Firefighter Health and Safety Act (S.5433, with House companion H.R.10492) was introduced September 17 and referred to Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs.
