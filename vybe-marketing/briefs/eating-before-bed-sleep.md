Title (56 characters): How Long Before Bed Should You Stop Eating? The Evidence
Meta (158 characters): There's no proven cut-off for your last meal before bed. See what studies of late eating and sleep show, where they disagree, and how to read your own nights.

## Answer first (40 to 60 words)

No number of hours is proven: surveys link eating near bedtime with worse sleep in students and older adults (Chung 2020; Chong 2026), and the reverse in night-shift nurses (Park 2023). Two small trials disagree (Enomoto 2026; Duan 2021). Your own nights can show a pattern, not a cause, and wrist sleep estimates differ from lab recordings (Lee 2025).

(59 words by `wc -w`. No product, no rule, no clock time. Every sentence carries a source, because this is the line most likely to be quoted. This is the AI Overview candidate: it agrees with the Overview that timing may matter and adds what the Overview leaves out, how strong the evidence is and who it was measured in.)

## Outline

**H2 1. How long before bed should you stop eating?**
- The answer-first paragraph above.
- One line on where "two to three hours" comes from: it is common sleep-hygiene advice. Studies have used three hours (Chung 2020) and one hour against five (Enomoto 2026; Duan 2021) as their test gaps. None of them established a threshold.
- Say plainly that this page offers no meal-timing rule, and why: the evidence doesn't support one.

**H2 2. Does eating late affect sleep? What the studies show** (target for "does eating late affect sleep", "eating before bed", "eating before bed myth")
Label the strength of each piece in the copy itself ("a survey found", "a small trial found"):
- *Associations, many people.* In 793 Australian university students, eating within three hours of bedtime went with more night waking. It did not go with taking longer to fall asleep or sleeping less (Chung 2020, cross-sectional). In 3,463 Dutch adults with a mean age of 64, a longer gap between the last meal and bedtime went with better self-reported sleep quality (Chong 2026, cross-sectional). A 2026 systematic review of 57 studies in college students found that 15 studies related meal timing to sleep. Later last meals went with poorer sleep health, but most studies were cross-sectional, and the authors say trials are needed to know which way the link runs (Almahasneh 2026).
- *Trials, few people.* Thirteen healthy young women ate dinner 1 hour or 5 hours before their usual bedtime for four nights each, at home. Home sleep recordings showed shorter sleep, lower sleep efficiency and more waking after the late dinners (Enomoto 2026, randomised crossover). Twenty healthy adults ate the same dinner 1 hour or 5 hours before bed for one night each in a sleep lab. Conventional sleep stages did not differ, and the late dinner went with deeper sleep early in the night and lighter sleep later (Duan 2021, post hoc analysis of a randomised crossover study).
- The line to write: the surveys of students and older adults point one way, the survey of night-shift nurses points the other (H2 6), and the two small trials point different ways. Nobody has shown how many hours matter, or for whom.
- Why surveys can't settle it: people who eat late often also go to bed later, eat larger dinners, drink, or have social evenings. A survey can't separate those. (This sets up H2 7.)

**H2 3. Can I sleep 1 hour after eating?** (PAA)
- Both trials above tested exactly this gap. One found worse sleep continuity after four nights; the other found no adverse change in conventional sleep stages after one night. So the honest answer is "the evidence is split", not "yes" or "no".
- If late meals regularly leave you uncomfortable at night, that is worth raising with a clinician. Name no condition.

**H2 4. Is it better to go to bed hungry or eat late?** (PAA)
- Say plainly that no study cited here compared going to bed hungry with eating late, so the page cannot answer the question either way. Don't stretch a nearby study to answer it.
- Nearby evidence, described only as what it tested:
  - A retrospective study of 14 college soccer players over a season compared nights of higher vs lower pre-sleep intake and found no difference in sleep duration, sleep disturbances, overnight HRV or resting heart rate (Greenwalt 2023). It compared amounts eaten before sleep, not hunger against a late meal. Two of its authors work for WHOOP; say so on the page.
  - A randomised crossover trial in 24 young women tested meal size: a larger share of the day's food at dinner went with a lower parasympathetic HRV index during sleep, measured with a lab heart recorder (Tada 2018). It changed how much was eaten at dinner, not when. Write it that way, and don't compare size with timing.
- No calorie targets and no snack lists. Snack-list intent belongs to other pages.

**H2 5. What is the 3-2-1 rule for sleeping?** (PAA)
- Describe it as a popular rule of thumb for the hours before bed (food, then work, then screens), not as a finding.
- Its food step is the same two-to-three-hour advice as H2 1, and the evidence for that step is the mixed picture in H2 2. Don't evaluate the work and screen steps here; they are outside Nourish.

**H2 6. Should I eat at 2am if I'm hungry? What if I work nights?** (PAA)
- For people sleeping after a night shift, the pattern can run the other way. In 128 nurses after a rotating night shift, a shorter gap between the meal and sleep went with longer measured sleep and better self-rated sleep (Park 2023, cross-sectional).
- The point for the reader: the "stop eating early" advice was built around night-time sleep and may not carry over to daytime sleep. Give no shift-work rule.

**H2 7. What is the ideal timing for meals? Does a shorter eating window help sleep?** (PAA; "meal timing" 1,600)
- Keep to sleep. A 2024 systematic review of six randomised trials of time-restricted eating (at least 14 hours a day without food, for at least 8 weeks) found it "does not typically worsen sleep parameters". Results varied: one trial reported better self-rated sleep and others reported lower sleep efficiency (Bohlman 2024).
- Say that no trial cited here has found an ideal meal schedule for sleep (Bohlman 2024 found no consistent change across six trials). Write no metabolism, blood-sugar or weight content, even though the "meal timing" results page is full of it.

**H2 8. How to read late dinners in your own data**
- HRV and resting heart rate move with training, alcohol, menstrual-cycle phase and being unwell. HRV is sensitive, not specific (Altini and Plews 2021). A lower night after a late dinner has more than one possible explanation.
- Wrist-measured sleep stages are estimates, and consumer trackers differ from lab recordings on total sleep, efficiency and wake time (Lee 2025). Read trends across nights, not one night's stage chart.
- A fair comparison changes one thing:
  - keep bedtime the same;
  - move only dinner time;
  - give each pattern several nights;
  - compare against your own usual range.
- When the nights differ, list what else changed (bedtime, meal size, a drink, a hard session) as possible explanations, not one cause. Use "associated with" throughout.
- This section hands off to the one Vybe section.
