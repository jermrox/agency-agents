**T1. Someone complaining about a wearable subscription**

> Fair frustration. The research we've read says the objection isn't only the price, it's feeling like you rent access to your own data. Worth asking any brand: can I export everything, in what format, and what stays if I cancel?

**T2. Someone asking whether any option has no subscription**

> A few do now (Polar, Garmin's CIRQA, Amazfit). We're building one too at Vybe Health, pre-launch, no required subscription. Honest caveat: we have no ship date yet, so compare the ones you can buy today first.

**T3. Someone asking whether HRV is worth tracking**

> Worth it if you read the trend, not the day. One morning's HRV moves with training, alcohol, cycle phase, a cold. A 7-day rolling average against your own range is what the sports-science literature uses (Plews et al., Sports Med 2013).

**T4. Someone worried about one low HRV reading**

> One low reading isn't a verdict. HRV is sensitive but not specific: it drops for many reasons at once (Altini & Plews, Sensors 2021, ~9M readings). Look at the week, note what changed, and if you feel unwell, that beats any number.

**T5. Someone asking why their deep sleep varies so much**

> Two things. Deep sleep really does vary night to night with training, alcohol, timing and age. And a wrist device estimates stages from movement and heart rate, not brain waves, so the estimate itself wobbles. Trust weeks, not nights.

**T6. Someone asking whether their tracker's sleep stages are accurate**

> They're estimates. Wrist trackers do well at sleep vs. wake and worse at staging compared with lab polysomnography (Chinoy et al., Sleep 2021; Lee et al., JCSM 2025 meta-analysis). Good for your own patterns, not for medical decisions.

**T7. A developer asking about wearable APIs**

> Depends what you need. Aggregators (Terra, Junction) give you many brands' processed data via one API. Brand APIs give you one brand, usually behind a membership. Raw or near-raw sensor access from a consumer band is still rare.

**T8. A developer asking who lets you build on the hardware itself**

> Rare. Ultrahuman has opened sensor streams. At Vybe Health we're designing the band to be built on, with access the owner grants by consent, starting at interpretation, not signal cleaning. Pre-launch; looking for design partners. — John

**T9. Someone asking which wearable to buy**

> Honest answer: pick by the question you want answered. Training load: a sports watch. Sleep trends: any ring or band, but treat stages as estimates. If "who owns my data" matters, read the privacy policy before the spec sheet.

**T10. Someone asking who owns their health data**

> In the US, most consumer wearable data sits outside HIPAA. The FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule covers "health apps and similar technologies not covered by HIPAA." Beyond that, your rights depend on the brand's policy and your state.

**T11. Someone saying their watch "detected" a condition or proved a cause**

> Glad you're paying attention. One caution: a consumer wearable shows a pattern, not a cause, and most aren't cleared to detect any condition. If something feels off, the reading is a good reason to see a clinician, not a verdict. — John

**T12. Someone asking what Vybe is or when it ships**

> Vybe Health: a screenless band designed to read five signals against your own baseline, with answers that say how sure they are. No required subscription. Pre-launch, no ship date yet; we'd rather say "not yet" than guess. — John

### Thread 1: Who owns your health data

1/ Your wearable knows when you slept, how hard you trained and how your heart behaved all night. Who owns that record? Shorter answer than you'd think, and it depends on where you live. A thread on what to ask before you buy.

2/ In the US, HIPAA covers doctors, insurers and their vendors. Most consumer wearables are none of those. So the law most people assume protects their sleep data usually does not apply to it. (HHS, HIPAA for professionals: https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/index.html)

3/ What does apply: the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule, amended in 2024 to make clear it covers "health apps and similar technologies not covered by HIPAA." It forces breach notices. It does not stop a company selling your data. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule

4/ Some states go further. Washington's My Health My Data Act (RCW 19.373) requires consent to collect and share consumer health data and bans selling it without a signed authorization. https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373

5/ In the EU and UK, GDPR Article 20 gives you the right to receive your data "in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format" and move it to another service. Portability is a right there, a feature everywhere else. https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/

6/ So, four questions for any brand before you buy: Can I export everything, not a summary? In what format? What happens to my data if I cancel or the company changes hands? Is my data sold or used for ad targeting?

7/ Our answers at Vybe Health, from the policy we publish: personal health data is never sold; it is shared only with your consent, with service providers that run the service, or where the law requires. And in its words: "We do not use your data for advertising targeting."

8/ We're designing the band so the data you buy it to collect stays yours to read. Pre-launch, so hold us to those four questions when it ships. If your current device answers them well, keep it. If it won't answer, that is the answer.

### Thread 2: What a personal baseline is

1/ "Your HRV is 45. Is that good?" No one can answer that about you from one number. Here is why sports scientists stopped comparing athletes to tables and started comparing them to themselves.

2/ HRV, resting heart rate and sleep differ enormously between healthy people. A number that is low for one person is normal for another. The population table tells you where you sit among strangers. It does not tell you whether today is different for you.

3/ So the field moved to within-person tracking. Plews and colleagues (Sports Med, 2013) argued for weekly or 7-day rolling averages of HRV in elite endurance athletes, because day-to-day noise hides the real trend. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8

4/ Buchheit (Frontiers in Physiology, 2014) went further: heart-rate measures only mean something when read against the person's own context, training load and history. No single road leads to Rome. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2014.00073

5/ The biggest dataset, about 9 million readings from 28,175 people (Altini & Plews, Sensors 2021), shows HRV and resting HR shift with training, alcohol, menstrual-cycle phase and illness. Same number, many possible reasons. https://doi.org/10.3390/s21237932

6/ A personal baseline is your own normal range, built from your own nights and mornings, and it moves: through a training block, a season, a life change. "Higher than your own normal" is a different sentence from "above average for your age."

7/ It also takes time. Three nights tell you little. Two weeks start a range. Six weeks give you something to trust. Any product that gives you a confident verdict on day one is reading a table, not you.

8/ Vybe Health's band is designed to read five signals against your own changing baseline, and to say how sure it is: "moderate confidence, 19 days of data" rather than a bare score. Pre-launch. Until then, the 7-day average on whatever you wear now is a good start.

### Thread 3: Why five signals beat one score

1/ A readiness score is one number built from several signals, weighted by rules you cannot see. It feels precise. It hides two things: how sure the system is, and what it could not see. A thread on why we'd rather show you five signals than one score.

2/ Start with the signal most scores lean on: HRV. It is sensitive: it moves when something changes. It is not specific: it does not tell you what changed. Training, alcohol, cycle phase and illness all move it (Altini & Plews, Sensors 2021). https://doi.org/10.3390/s21237932

3/ Sleep is the second pillar of most scores, and alcohol shows how the signals interact. A 2024 meta-analysis of 27 studies (Gardiner et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews) found alcohol delays and shortens REM sleep from about two drinks, worse with dose. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030

4/ So one late drink can lower sleep quality, lower HRV and raise resting heart rate at once. A single score adds those together and reports "low recovery." It cannot tell you that one evening, not your training, is the likeliest reason.

5/ Five signals read side by side can. Sleep (Restore), training load (Move), food and drink timing (Nourish), time with people and alone (Connect), heart rate and HRV (Vitals). When several move together after one event, the pattern points somewhere. It still is not proof.

6/ The honest answer names more than one explanation. Prototype, sample data, from our design work at Vybe Health: "Your HRV is below your own range for the second morning. Short sleep fits most closely; Tuesday's hard session fits too. Moderate confidence: 19 days of data."

7/ What that sentence does that a score cannot: it ranks explanations, counts the evidence, and leaves the final call to you. Vybe's band and app are designed to answer that way and to say what they could not see. Pre-launch; we're looking for design partners to build on it.

### Thread 4: How wearable sleep estimates work, and what they cannot see

1/ Your band said 1h 12m of deep sleep. Where did that number come from? Not from your brain. A thread on how wrist devices estimate sleep stages, how close they get, and what no wrist can see.

2/ The gold standard is polysomnography: electrodes on the scalp read brain waves, sensors read eye movement and muscle tone, and a trained scorer labels every 30 seconds. Sleep stages are defined by brain activity. A wrist has no view of it.

3/ A wrist device sees movement, heart rate, heart-rate variability, sometimes skin temperature and blood oxygen. An algorithm trained against lab recordings guesses the stage from those proxies. The output is an estimate, every night.

4/ How good an estimate? A 2021 study tested seven consumer devices against polysomnography (Chinoy et al., Sleep). They did well at telling sleep from wake and less well at telling stages apart. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa291

5/ A 2025 meta-analysis of 24 studies (Lee et al., J Clin Sleep Med) found wrist trackers differ significantly from the lab on total sleep time, efficiency, time to fall asleep and time awake after sleep onset. Useful for patterns, not for precision. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.11460

6/ Researchers who use wearables know this. A 2019 review in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (de Zambotti et al.) set out how to use them well: for trends over many nights, with their limits stated. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001947

7/ What no wrist can see: why you woke, what you drank, whether you feel rested, and anything that needs a clinician. A consumer device is not a diagnostic tool. If sleep worries you, the device is a reason to talk to a sleep physician, not a replacement for one.

8/ At Vybe Health we call sleep stages estimates, in the product and in public, and we're designing the Restore signal to be read across weeks, not one night. Pre-launch. Whatever you wear tonight: trust the month, question the minute.
